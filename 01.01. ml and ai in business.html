Case brief: the Duolingo "AI-first" memo

On April 28, 2025, Duolingo's CEO, Luis von Ahn, posted an internal all-hands email on the company's LinkedIn page announcing that Duolingo would be an "AI-first" company. The memo said the company would gradually stop using contractors for work that AI can handle, that AI use would count in hiring and in performance reviews, and that a team would receive new headcount only if it could not automate more of its work. Public reaction was strongly negative; several weeks later von Ahn published a clarification stating that the company was still hiring at the same pace and that AI was meant to accelerate the work of employees rather than replace them. In 2026 he said the company had dropped AI usage as a formal performance-review measure. In the same week as the memo, the company announced 148 new language courses produced with generative-AI tooling, roughly doubling its catalogue.

Each of today's six sub-topics closes one part of the case: which family each of Duolingo's learning systems belongs to (1), which contractor tasks a rule, a model or a bought product should take over (2), how the content-generation ambition should have been written as a defined problem (3), what "the model learns" actually means for a churn model (4), what a churn model would actually consume (5), and how a churn or ticket classifier turns a probability into a business decision (6). The case section at the end assembles the six answers.

The three families of machine learning, and the questions that tell you which one a business problem needs

Supervised, unsupervised and reinforcement learning defined by the signal each one learns from; three routing questions applied to Duolingo's systems.

A. Lesson notes with slide mapping

5:00 to 7:00

Machine learning, abbreviated ML, is a set of methods for building a decision procedure from recorded examples instead of writing the procedure by hand. A model is a mathematical function with adjustable numbers, its parameters. Training adjusts the parameters so that the function's outputs match a target on the recorded examples; applying the trained function to new cases is inference, or prediction. The definition does not say that the machine understands anything, does not say that the procedure is correct (only that the outputs match the target on the available examples), and does not say which function is used: a two-parameter line and a language model with billions of parameters are both covered. Artificial intelligence, abbreviated AI, is a broader and less precise word; whenever a company says "AI", ask which family of machine learning does the work, or whether the system is a set of hand-written rules.

7:00 to 11:00

The three families differ in the signal they learn from. Supervised learning learns from examples that already carry the correct answer, the label. Every past Duolingo exercise has a label (right or wrong), so Birdbrain is supervised; churn prediction (stayed or left, known a year later) and ticket routing (tags written by agents) have the same shape.

Unsupervised learning learns from the inputs alone and looks for structure: clustering finds groups of similar records (types of learners nobody labelled); anomaly detection finds records unlike the rest (unusual account activity when confirmed fraud cases are too few for a supervised model); dimensionality reduction compresses many variables into a few summary variables.

Reinforcement learning is different in kind: an agent chooses an action, the environment responds, and the agent receives a reward; its rule for choosing actions is its policy, and training raises the total reward over time. Duolingo's reminder system chooses one of several texts per learner each evening, and a return within two hours is the reward. Duolingo's 2020 paper describes it as a variant of a multi-armed bandit, the simplest reinforcement method, and reports about 0.5 percent more daily active users and 2 percent higher new-user retention.

11:00 to 15:00

To route a business problem, ask three questions in a fixed order and stop at the first yes. Question A: does the system choose actions repeatedly, with feedback that depends on the action chosen? Then reinforcement learning. This question comes first because such a system only ever sees the outcome of the action it took: the data is produced by its own decisions, which breaks the supervised-learning assumption that examples arrive independently of the model. Question B: if the system does not act, do past cases already carry the correct answer? Then supervised learning; the answer may be a number or a category (Sub-topic 6). Question C: if there are no labels, is the aim to discover structure nobody has labelled? Then unsupervised learning. Three times no: not a machine-learning problem, but perhaps a rule, a report or a management decision (Sub-topic 2).

Two remarks. A large language model, abbreviated LLM, is trained by self-supervised learning: the label is the next word of a text, so labels are manufactured from the text itself and nobody tags anything; mechanically it is supervised learning, economically it is something a company buys or licenses, because training one costs tens of millions of dollars. And one company usually needs all three families at once: the example spreads eight Duolingo problems across the three columns.

15:00 to 17:00

Three misunderstandings to avoid. "Supervised" does not mean that a person watches the training; it means that labels exist. "Unsupervised" does not mean autonomous or unaccountable; it means that no labels exist, and the results still need a person to interpret them. Reinforcement learning is not "the advanced one"; it fits one specific problem shape, and for most business problems supervised learning is both the appropriate choice and the cheapest.

C. Real-life example: routing eight Duolingo problems to the right family

Task
Take eight concrete problems that Duolingo faces and, for each one, decide whether it needs supervised, unsupervised or reinforcement learning by asking the three routing questions in order.
Relation
This is Sub-topic 1 applied: the family is decided by the available signal (labels, structure or rewards), not by the algorithm. The example settles the first part of the case: what kind of "AI" each of Duolingo's systems is.

Imagine you are a business analyst who has just joined Duolingo's data team a month after the memo. Your manager hands you a list of eight requests from different departments and asks for a first opinion on each: what kind of system would it need, and what data would that system consume? The list contains Birdbrain's prediction of whether a learner will answer the next exercise correctly; a marketing request to group learners by usage pattern; the evening reminder system; a finance request to estimate the minutes each learner will practise next week; a trust-and-safety request to spot unusual account activity; a support request to route billing tickets automatically; a product request to order the exercises inside a session so that more sessions are completed; and an analytics request to compress two hundred usage variables into five.

The program below does what you would do at your desk: for each request it asks question A, then B, then C, stops at the first yes, and places the request in the matching column. Watch the log: the reminder system and the exercise ordering go to reinforcement learning because the system acts and observes the consequences of its own choice; Birdbrain, the minutes estimate and the ticket routing go to supervised learning because labels already exist; the grouping, the anomaly search and the compression go to unsupervised learning because nobody has labelled the answer. The result is exported as a spreadsheet, which is exactly the format of the Card Sort assignment.

D. Code illustration of the example

Task
A short JavaScript program (it runs directly in this page) that stores the eight problems with the honest answers to the three questions, applies the decision procedure to each problem, draws the result on a three-column board, and exports the routing as a CSV file.
Relation
Every line corresponds to a step of Sub-topic 1. The function decideFamily is the three questions in code; the board on the right is Slide V2 filled in with the Duolingo case.
The page provides four helpers to every example: await pause(message) prints the message in the execution window and waits so you can read the step; log(message) prints without waiting; viz draws on the canvas with viz.clear(), viz.rect(x, y, width, height, fill, stroke), viz.circle(x, y, radius, fill), viz.line(x1, y1, x2, y2, colour, width) and viz.text(x, y, text, {size, color, align, bold}) on a 720 by 360 grid; saveText(fileName, text) offers a download button for a file the program produced.
Step-by-step explanation of the program
  1. The list problems holds the eight requests. Each entry records the answers to the three questions as true or false; these answers are facts about the business situation, written down by the analyst, not something the computer discovers.
  2. questions stores the exact wording of questions A, B and C so the execution window can quote them.
  3. decideFamily is the routing procedure. It checks A first (acts and gets rewards), then B (labels exist), then C (structure wanted). It returns the family name, or "not a learning problem" if all three answers are no.
  4. columns and placed describe the drawing: one column per family, with a counter of how many cards have been placed in it.
  5. drawBoard clears the canvas and draws the three column headers with the signal each family needs; placeCard draws one request card in the next free row of its column.
  6. The main loop takes each problem, prints the answers to the questions actually asked (B is only asked when A was no, C only when A and B were no), calls decideFamily, draws the card, and pauses so you can check the answer against the three questions.
  7. After the loop the program counts the cards per column and prints the tally: three supervised, three unsupervised, two reinforcement.
  8. Finally it builds a CSV text with one line per problem and calls saveText, which adds a download button under the execution window.

Sub-topic 2. 17:00 to 29:00. Slides V3 and V4. Module 1.

When a simple rule is enough and machine learning is not worth it; build, buy or partner

A decision test for rule versus model, the arithmetic of whether extra accuracy pays for itself, and weighted criteria for build, buy or partner.

A. Lesson notes with slide mapping

17:00 to 20:00

A rule-based system is a decision procedure written by a person as explicit conditions (if the ticket contains "refund", send it to billing). Rules are transparent, cheap (no data needed) and stable (they change only when someone changes them). They fail when the conditions are many, change often, or cannot be put into words at all, such as "this exercise is at the right difficulty for this learner". Google's engineering guide "Rules of Machine Learning" (Martin Zinkevich) opens with Rule 1: do not be afraid to launch a product without machine learning. A heuristic (a rule of thumb written from experience) usually delivers a large share of the value immediately, while the data a model needs is still being collected. Rule 3: once the rules have become too complicated to maintain, switch to a model, because a model trained on data is easier to keep up to date than a hundred interacting conditions.

20:00 to 24:00

The decision test for any proposal. Question 1: can a person write the decision as a few stable conditions that reach the accuracy the business needs? If yes, write the rule, ship it and measure it; the measurement is the baseline, the number any future model must beat on the business metric. Question 2, if the rule falls short: is the extra accuracy worth its cost? Errors avoided per month times the cost of one error, against the one-off cost of labelled data and engineering plus the monthly cost of running, monitoring and retraining; the one-off cost divided by the net monthly benefit is the payback period. In the example (rule 90 percent, model 95 percent, 40,000 tickets a month, three minutes of agent time per misroute) the payback is under six months; at 4,000 tickets a month it would take years, so the decision depends on volume and error cost, not on accuracy alone. Question 3: do labelled examples exist, or can the rule produce them? Every agent correction of the rule's routing is a labelled example, so the sequence is: rule, measure, collect the corrections, then decide about a model.

24:00 to 28:00

If a model is justified, there are three ways to obtain it. Build: your own team trains and operates the model on your data; full control and any advantage it creates stay with you; the price is time, scarce skills and the risk of failing at one of the six stages. Buy: a product that already does the task (help-desk software with a built-in ticket classifier); fast and cheap to start, little skill needed, and every competitor can buy the same. Partner: a vendor adapts its system to your data, or you license a foundation model and add your own data and rules; Duolingo's conversational practice is a partner case.

Slide V4 scores the options on five weighted criteria, each option rated 1 to 5: strategic differentiation (0.30), speed to value (0.25), total cost over two years including people (0.20), control of data and model behaviour, including privacy obligations (0.15), in-house skills needed (0.10). Score = sum of weight times rating. The criteria stay fixed across projects; the ratings change. Ticket routing does not differentiate Duolingo, so buying wins; Birdbrain is the product itself, so building wins, which is what Duolingo did.

28:00 to 29:00

Read with this sub-topic, "gradually stop using contractors for work that AI can handle" is a claim that, for certain tasks, a bought or partnered generative model plus a review step beats the current baseline at lower cost. It is testable task by task: the baseline error rate of a human translator, the error rate of generated text after review, the cost of one error that reaches a learner, the cost of the review step. The memo published none of these numbers; part of the backlash came from readers who suspected that nobody had computed them.

C. Real-life example: should the support team route billing tickets with a rule, a model, or a product?

Task
Measure a keyword rule on twenty labelled support tickets, compute whether a model's extra accuracy would pay for itself at Duolingo's ticket volume, and score build, buy and partner for that model.
Relation
This is the decision test of Slide V3 and the scoring table of Slide V4 applied to one of the contractor-handled tasks the memo refers to: first-line support triage.

Duolingo's support team receives tens of thousands of tickets a month. A share of them concern payments: double charges, refunds, cancelled subscriptions, family plans. Routing those to the billing team quickly matters because billing complaints escalate to chargebacks. The current process is a rule written by a support lead: if the ticket contains any of eight money-related words, it goes to billing. Nobody has ever measured how good that rule is.

You take twenty recent tickets that agents have already routed by hand, so the correct team is known, and you run the rule on them. It gets eighteen right. It misses a ticket about a free trial ending early because "trial" is not on the list, and it wrongly sends a ticket about a missing story card to billing because "card" is. Ninety percent. A colleague proposes a machine-learning classifier and estimates ninety-five percent. Is the difference worth it? At forty thousand tickets a month, the rule misroutes four thousand and the model two thousand; each misroute costs three minutes of agent time; the saving is about 3,600 euros a month against a one-off cost of about 17,400 euros for labelling and engineering plus 500 euros a month of upkeep. Payback: under six months. Yes, but ship the rule now and log every correction, because those corrections are the labels. Then the third question: build, buy or partner? Ticket routing does not differentiate Duolingo from anyone, so on the weighted criteria a bought help-desk classifier scores highest. Had the task been Birdbrain, the differentiation rating would have been five out of five and building would have won. The program runs those three steps.

D. Code illustration of the example

Task
Apply a keyword rule to twenty labelled tickets, fill a confusion matrix, compute accuracy, precision and recall, compute the payback period of a model from volume and cost assumptions, and compute weighted build, buy and partner scores; draw the matrix, the accuracy bars and the score bars.
Relation
The rule is the baseline of Slide V3; the payback arithmetic is the second question on V3; the scoring loop is Slide V4 in code.
Step-by-step explanation of the program
  1. tickets holds twenty ticket texts with a human label (billing: true or false). Ticket 11 has no keyword and ticket 14 has a misleading one, so the rule will make exactly two mistakes.
  2. keywords is the rule; ruleSaysBilling lower-cases the text and checks whether any keyword occurs in it.
  3. cm is the confusion matrix: true positives, abbreviated TP,, false positives, abbreviated FP,, false negatives, abbreviated FN, and true negatives, abbreviated TN,. The loop over the tickets compares the rule's answer with the label and increases the matching counter; mistakes pause the run.
  4. Accuracy is (TP + TN) divided by all tickets; precision is TP divided by everything the rule called billing; recall is TP divided by all real billing tickets. The model's ninety-five percent is an estimate entered by hand.
  5. Step 2 converts accuracy into money: misroutes per month with the rule and with the model, saving per month, one-off cost, upkeep, and the payback period in months. worthIt applies the lecture's decision rule (payback under 12 months and at least three points of gain).
  6. Step 3 stores the criteria weights and a rating from 1 to 5 for each option on each criterion; the loop computes the weighted sum, redraws the bars and pauses after every option.
  7. The best option is the highest score; the closing message explains that for Birdbrain the differentiation rating would change and so would the winner.
  8. The ratings and scores are exported as a CSV so students can change the weights in a spreadsheet and see when the recommendation flips.

The six-stage machine-learning workflow, and why most projects fail at problem definition rather than at the algorithm

Problem definition, data collection, data preparation, model training, evaluation, deployment and monitoring; the feedback loops between them; a five-question checklist for stage 1.

A. Lesson notes with slide mapping

29:00 to 33:00

A machine-learning project runs through six stages, each of which can send the project back to an earlier one. The sequence was published in 1999 as CRISP-DM and is still in use. Stage 1, problem definition: which decision changes, what is predicted, which metric must move, what the current process achieves. Stage 2, data collection: which records contain the target, who owns them, are there enough labelled examples. Stage 3, data preparation: cleaning, joining and aggregating into a table with one row per unit and one column per feature (Sub-topic 5). Stage 4, model training: a loss function and the training loop (Sub-topic 4). Stage 5, evaluation: measuring on data the model never saw and translating errors into business cost. Stage 6, deployment and monitoring: serving the predictions and watching accuracy and data drift over time.

The dashed arrows on the overview slide are the feedback loops: evaluation finds a wrongly computed feature (back to stage 3); deployment finds a changed goal, or data that no longer resembles the training data (back to stage 1); data collection finds that the target was never recorded (back to stage 1). A plan that draws the six stages as a straight line with dates has not met reality yet.

33:00 to 37:00

Practitioner surveys consistently report that the stage at which most projects are abandoned is problem definition, followed by data (target never recorded, data inaccessible, labels too few or inconsistent); failures in model training are rare, because algorithms are the mature, documented part of the field. The classroom simulation uses stop probabilities of 35, 20, 12, 5, 10 and 15 percent for the six stages: a teaching assumption that reproduces the pattern, not measured statistics.

Stage 1 is dangerous because it can be skipped without anyone noticing. "Use AI to create course content faster" sounds like a problem definition but is a wish: no decision, no target, no metric, no baseline, no data. A stage-1 document is complete when five questions have written answers: (1) which decision will the prediction change, who makes it, how often; (2) what exactly is predicted, the target, for which unit (the row: a learner, a ticket, a lesson), at which moment; (3) which business metric must move, by how much, by when; (4) what the current baseline is and what one error costs; (5) which data records the target today and who owns access to it.

37:00 to 41:00

Applied to the memo's content programme: decision, for each new lesson, whether a machine-drafted lesson goes to review as it is, needs rework, or is rejected; target, the number of reviewer corrections a draft will need, predicted when the draft is produced; metric, reviewer hours per published lesson down 40 percent within two quarters, with error reports per lesson not rising (the guard rail that prevents success by publishing bad lessons quickly); baseline, the fully human authoring process, and the cost of one error that reaches learners (a re-publication plus a drop in lesson ratings); data, the reviewers' change-log of the last eighteen months, owned by the learning team, in which every draft and its final version is a labelled example.

The wish became a supervised-learning problem with a target, a metric, a guard rail and a data owner, and it became testable within six months. Note where the labels come from: the contractors' corrections. A company that stops using the people who produce the labels before it has collected enough of them has removed stage 2 of its own project. The simulation sends 100 projects through the six stages, applies the checklist, which lowers the stage-1 stop probability to 10 percent, and runs again with nothing else changed.

C. Real-life example: one hundred projects through six stages, before and after a checklist

Task
Simulate a portfolio of one hundred machine-learning projects passing through the six stages, count where they stop, then apply the five-question checklist to stage 1 and repeat; finally write the stage-1 document for Duolingo's content-generation project.
Relation
This makes the claim of the sub-topic visible: the stage with the highest stop probability is problem definition, and improving only that stage raises the number of projects that reach production more than any algorithmic improvement could.

Picture the head of data at a company like Duolingo a year after the memo. Forty teams have started projects with the words "AI-first" in their charters. The head of data wants to know where those projects are dying and what one change would save the most of them. She does not need a model for that; she needs a simulation of her portfolio with honest stop probabilities per stage, and a policy she can enforce.

The program plays her portfolio twice. In the baseline run, projects stop at stage 1 with probability thirty-five percent, and only about a third reach production. In the second run the only change is that no project may leave stage 1 without written answers to the five checklist questions, which lowers the stage-1 stop probability to ten percent; the number reaching production rises by roughly a third. Then the program does what the head of data would do with the memo's most visible project: it writes the five answers for "produce course content with generative AI" and turns the wish into a supervised-learning problem with a target, a metric, a guard rail and a data owner. The two funnels are exported as a spreadsheet.

How a model learns: training data, features and labels, the loss function, the training loop, and a first look at overfitting

The exact mechanics of training: which numbers change, in which direction, and why; then why a model can be perfect on its training data and useless on new data.

A. Lesson notes with slide mapping

41:00 to 45:00

Training starts with a table. The rows are the examples, one per unit (here, one learner). A feature is a measured property that is available when the prediction is made (lessons completed this week); the label is the correct answer recorded afterwards (minutes practised the following week). A table with features and labels is a training set. Slide V6 shows twelve learners with one feature and one label: eight form the training set, which the model may look at as often as it likes; four form the test set, locked away until training ends and used for one purpose, to measure the model's behaviour on learners it has never seen. A model evaluated on its own training data has not been evaluated.

The model is a straight line, minutes = w times lessons + b, with two parameters w and b that start at zero. "The model learned" means exactly that w and b moved from zero to values that bring the line close to the training points; in Birdbrain the same sentence holds with millions of numbers.

45:00 to 50:00

One iteration of the training loop: (1) compute a prediction for every training example with the current parameters; (2) compare each prediction with its label; (3) combine the differences into one number, the loss, using a loss function; (4) work out, for each parameter, the direction in which a small change reduces the loss, the gradient; (5) move each parameter a small step in that direction. Training repeats the loop until the loss stops falling or a fixed number of iterations is reached: eighty in the example, millions for a large language model, the same five steps.

The loop needs labels (step 2), a loss function (step 4) and numeric parameters (step 5). It needs no understanding of what lessons or minutes are; it would run identically on columns called x and y. All meaning enters through the columns, which is why feature engineering (Sub-topic 5) matters so much.

50:00 to 53:00

For a numeric label the standard loss is the mean squared error, abbreviated MSE,: prediction minus label, squared, averaged over the examples. Squaring removes the sign (ten minutes too many and ten too few count equally) and penalises large errors far more than small ones (an error of 10 contributes 100, an error of 1 contributes 1). If one badly wrong prediction costs the business far more than ten slightly wrong ones, that is the right choice; if every minute of error costs the same, the mean absolute error, abbreviated MAE,, which averages the plain differences, is the better loss. The loop minimises whatever it is given, so the choice of loss is a business choice: the model will be exactly as wrong as the loss allowed it to be. For categorical labels the loss is log-loss (Sub-topic 6).

53:00 to 57:00

Steps 4 and 5 are gradient descent. For a line with squared error, the gradient for w is the average of 2 times error times feature, and for b the average of 2 times error; the gradient points where the loss rises fastest, so each parameter moves the opposite way: new w = old w minus learning rate times gradient, and likewise for b. The learning rate is the step size, chosen by hand before training; values chosen before training rather than learned are hyperparameters. Too large: the parameters overshoot, the loss oscillates or grows, training diverges. Too small: each iteration changes almost nothing. The example uses 0.008, found by trying a few values, which is how practitioners find it. Healthy training shows a loss curve that drops steeply and then flattens. An epoch is one pass through the whole training set; when the table is too large, each iteration uses a random subset, a batch (stochastic gradient descent); with eight rows every iteration is an epoch.

57:00 to 61:00

After eighty iterations the line has a training loss of about 17 (predictions off by roughly four minutes on average) and about the same loss on the four held-out learners: the model generalises. A polynomial of degree seven, with eight parameters for eight training points, passes through every training point exactly: training loss zero, test loss in the thousands, because between the points it reproduces the random fluctuations of the labels as if they were law. That is overfitting: the model fits the training examples, noise included, so closely that it stops describing the underlying relationship and fails on anything it has not memorised.

Three consequences. Training loss is not a measure of usefulness; only the loss on unseen data is. A perfect training score is a warning, not an achievement: ask for the test set. Overfitting is more likely with many parameters per example, noisy labels and long training; the defences are more data, simpler models, regularisation (penalising large parameter values) and stopping when the test loss starts to rise. Slide V10 shows the curve: training loss keeps falling with model flexibility, test loss falls and then rises, and the right flexibility is where the test loss is lowest. For Duolingo: a churn model trained only on Spanish learners can fit them well and mispredict every other course.

Feature engineering: turning raw business data into inputs a model can use

Why the model never sees the raw records; five kinds of transformation; the leakage check; what makes a feature good.

A. Lesson notes with slide mapping

61:00 to 64:00

A company's data does not arrive as a training table. The app records an event log: one row per event (a lesson completed, a notification opened, a payment) with a timestamp, a learner identifier and a few fields; an active learner produces hundreds of rows a year. A churn model needs one row per learner. Feature engineering is the design and computation of the columns a model will consume from the raw records a business already has. Practitioners report that more than half of a project's working time goes here, in stage 3, and almost none on the choice of algorithm, because the training loop attaches no meaning to its columns: a model cannot discover that "days since the last lesson" predicts churn unless someone computes that column.

64:00 to 68:00

Five transformations cover most feature engineering on event data. Counting over a window: lessons in the last 7 and the last 30 days (current habit versus history). Recency: days since the most recent lesson, usually the strongest churn predictor in subscription businesses and one line of computation. Aggregation: averages or totals of a numeric field across the learner's rows (mean lesson duration, mean share of correct answers, share of evening lessons extracted from the timestamps). Encoding of categories: "ios", "android" and "web" cannot be multiplied by a weight, so one-hot encoding replaces the text column by one 0/1 column per category (is-iOS, is-Android, is-Web) and the model learns a weight for each. Scaling: lessons, minutes and days differ in size by factors of hundreds, and gradient descent misbehaves when one column is a thousand times larger than another; min-max scaling maps a column to the interval 0 to 1, standardisation subtracts the mean and divides by the standard deviation (z-scores).

68:00 to 71:00

Leakage is the use, in a feature, of information that will not be available at the moment the prediction is made. A churn model that includes "days until the subscription ends" reads the answer from that column, scores almost perfectly on the test set and predicts nothing in production, where the column does not exist yet. Every feature must pass one check: at the moment the prediction is needed, could this value be computed from data that exists at that moment? In the example "now" is fixed at midnight on September 8, 2026, and every feature uses rows before that moment. A good feature is available at prediction time (the leakage check), stable (a feature whose meaning changes when the product changes, such as "number of gems", silently breaks the model), and explainable to the person who acts on the prediction ("no practice for 14 days and falling accuracy" can be acted on; "feature 37 is high" cannot). The Feature Table assignment asks for exactly the table the program produces, computed from a larger downloadable log, with a justification of each column against these three properties.

Regression versus classification: the output type decides the task; logistic regression as a classifier; business use cases

Predict a number or predict a category; the sigmoid, log-loss and the decision threshold; how the threshold turns a probability into a business decision; a map of use cases.

A. Lesson notes with slide mapping

71:00 to 73:30

Within supervised learning, one question separates two tasks: what kind of thing is the answer? A number on a scale (minutes, euros, days, units) makes it regression; one of a fixed set of categories (churned or stayed, billing or other, right or wrong, fraud or legitimate) makes it classification, binary classification with two classes and multi-class with more. The features do not decide: days since the last lesson and lessons in the last seven days can feed a regression for next week's minutes and a classification for churn. What changes is the label column, and with it the loss function and the use of the output: a regression output is read directly as the estimate; a classification output is a probability that a threshold turns into a decision. The first question for any "we need a model" request: is the answer a number or a category?

73:30 to 76:00

Linear regression outputs a weighted sum of the features plus a constant; with one feature it is the line of Sub-topic 4. Each weight says how much the prediction changes when its feature rises by one unit while the others stay fixed, so the weights are an explanation as well as a prediction, which is why linear regression is the first model to try for any business number: demand forecasting, price estimation, delivery time, practice minutes. More flexible models exist for relationships that are not straight lines; Sub-topic 4 stated the price of flexibility.

76:00 to 81:00

A weighted sum can be any number, but "churned" is a yes or no. Logistic regression passes the weighted sum z through the sigmoid, 1 / (1 + e to the power of minus z), which maps any number to a value strictly between 0 and 1 in an S shape: very negative inputs give values near 0, very positive inputs give values near 1, and 0 gives one half. The output is read as a probability that the learner belongs to the positive class, churned. Despite its name, logistic regression is a classification method; the name is historical (a regression on the logarithm of the odds).

Training is the loop of Sub-topic 4 with log-loss (cross-entropy) in place of squared error: minus the logarithm of the predicted churn probability for a learner who churned, minus the logarithm of one minus it for a learner who stayed. A confident wrong prediction produces a very large loss; a confident right one a loss near zero. The gradient for each weight is (prediction minus label) times the feature, so gradient descent proceeds as before. The trained weights are readable: positive on days since the last lesson (more days away, higher churn probability), negative on recent lessons. The line where the probability equals one half is the decision boundary: learners on one side are predicted to churn, on the other to stay.

81:00 to 84:00

A probability is not a decision. The company acts with a rule such as "if the probability of churn is above the threshold, include the learner in the win-back campaign", and the threshold is chosen from the cost of the two error types, not from statistics. A learner predicted to stay who churns is a false negative (a lost subscription, say 7 euros a month); a learner predicted to churn who would have stayed is a false positive (an unneeded discount, say 1 euro). With those costs, lowering the threshold from 0.5 to 0.3 flags more learners, catches more churners, sends more unneeded offers and, in the example, lowers the total cost; raise the cost of the offer to 10 euros and the calculation reverses. The model has not changed; the decision has. One model can serve several thresholds, one per campaign, because the costs differ.

The confusion matrix holds the counts of true positives, false positives, false negatives and true negatives at a threshold. Accuracy is the share of correct decisions; Precision is the share of predicted churners who really churned; Recall is the share of real churners the model caught. If 3 percent of learners churn, a model that predicts "stays" for everyone is 97 percent accurate and useless, with recall zero: ask for all three numbers, and for the costs.

84:00 to 85:00

Slide V16 maps common use cases onto the two tasks. Regression: demand and sales forecasting, price and cost estimation, delivery and processing times, lifetime value; for Duolingo, practice minutes or the expected length of a streak. Classification: churn, fraud, credit default, lead scoring, ticket routing, content moderation, defect detection; for Duolingo, Birdbrain's right-or-wrong prediction and the routing of billing tickets. Both tasks share the workflow of Sub-topic 3, the training loop of Sub-topic 4 and the feature table of Sub-topic 5.

Case: the Duolingo "AI-first" memo, assembled from the six sub-topics

The memo of April 28, 2025 (posted by Luis von Ahn on Duolingo's LinkedIn page; see the press coverage in the Links section) announced three policies: contractors would gradually be replaced for "work that AI can handle"; AI use would enter hiring and performance reviews; headcount would be granted only when a team could not automate further. The clarification of May 2025 kept the "AI-first" direction, stated that hiring continued at the same pace, and framed AI as a tool to accelerate employees. In August 2025 von Ahn said the memo "did not give enough context" and that no full-time employee had been laid off; in April 2026 he said the AI-usage criterion had been removed from performance reviews.

1. Which family is each system? Birdbrain: supervised (labels: right or wrong answers, generated by the product). Learner segmentation and anomaly detection: unsupervised. The evening reminder system: reinforcement learning, a bandit, because the system's own choice determines the feedback it sees. Generative course content: a self-supervised foundation model, mechanically supervised, economically bought or partnered.
2. Rule, model or product? Ticket triage: a keyword rule reaches about 90%, a model about 95%; at 40,000 tickets a month the model pays back in under six months, and a bought help-desk classifier scores highest on the weighted criteria. Translation and exercise drafting: a partnered generative model plus review; worth it only if the reviewed error rate and the review cost beat the human baseline, numbers the memo did not publish.
3. Stage 1 for the content programme. Decision: accept, rework or reject each machine-drafted lesson. Target: reviewer corrections per draft. Metric: reviewer hours per published lesson down 40% in two quarters with error reports not rising. Baseline: fully human authoring. Data: the reviewers' change-log, whose authors were the contractors. Removing the label source before collecting enough labels is a stage-2 failure built into the policy.
4. What "learns" means. For a retention model: the weights move by gradient descent until the loss on labelled learners is small; usefulness is the loss on learners never seen; a churn model trained on one course can be perfect on it and wrong everywhere else (overfitting).
5. What the model consumes. Not "the app log" but a feature table: counts over windows, recency, aggregates, one-hot platform, scaled columns, with a leakage check against the prediction moment. Most of the project's time goes here.
6. Where the human decision remains. A churn classifier outputs a probability; the threshold that turns it into a campaign list is set from the cost of a missed churner against the cost of an unneeded offer. "AI-first" does not remove that decision; it moves it to whoever owns the costs.

Reading the memo with this vocabulary: the announcement was an organisational policy about who does the work; it was not a defined machine-learning programme with targets, baselines, error costs and data owners. The public reaction concerned tone; the analytical gap concerned stage 1.

    Three families of machine learning, and three routing questions

    Supervised
    Signal: labels (recorded correct answers).
    Predict a number or a category.
    Duolingo: Birdbrain, churn, ticket routing.
    Unsupervised
    Signal: structure in the inputs.
    Clustering, anomaly detection, dimensionality reduction.

    Reinforcement
    Signal: rewards after the agent's own actions.

    V3

    Rule or model? The decision test

    • Question 1. Can a person write the decision as a few stable if-then conditions that reach the needed accuracy? Yes: ship the rule, measure it (the baseline), log corrections.
    • Question 2. Is the extra accuracy worth its cost? (errors avoided x cost per error) against (labelling + engineering + monthly upkeep). Payback period = one-off cost / net monthly benefit.
    • Question 3. Do labelled examples exist, or can the rule's corrections produce them within months?
    Google, Rules of Machine Learning, Rule 1: do not be afraid to launch a product without machine learning. Rule 3: choose machine learning over a complex heuristic.
    Ticket example: rule 90%, model 95%, 40,000 tickets/month, 3 minutes per misroute: payback under 6 months. At 4,000 tickets/month: years.

    The loss function: one number for how wrong the model is

    MSE = (1/n) x sum over examples of (prediction - label)^2

    • Squaring removes the sign and penalises large errors far more than small ones (error 10 counts 100, error 1 counts 1).
    • Mean absolute error averages the plain differences: every minute of error costs the same.
    • The choice of loss is a business choice: the model will be exactly as wrong as the loss allowed.
    • For categories (churned / stayed) the loss is log-loss: see V14.
    Worked row: learner practised 22 minutes, prediction 0 at the start, squared error 484; after training, prediction 20.4, squared error 2.6.
    V9

    Gradient descent: the update step and the learning rate

    w := w - learning rate x gradient(w)    b := b - learning rate x gradient(b)

    • For a line with squared error: gradient(w) = average of 2 x error x feature; gradient(b) = average of 2 x error.
    • The gradient points where the loss rises fastest; the parameters move the opposite way.
    • Learning rate (a hyperparameter): too large diverges, too small crawls. Example uses 0.008.
    • Iteration: one pass round the loop. Epoch: one pass through all rows. Batch: a random subset when the table is too large.
    Healthy training: loss drops steeply in the first iterations, then flattens.

    Feature engineering: from raw records to a feature table

    Raw event log
    one row per lesson
    timestamp, learner id, seconds, platform, accuracy

    Five transformations

    one row per learner

    Leakage: a feature that uses information unavailable at prediction time. Excellent test score, useless model. Check every column against "now".
    A good feature is available at prediction time, stable across product changes, and explainable to the person who acts on the prediction.
    V12

    Regression or classification: the output type decides

    Regression: the answer is a number on a scale
    minutes, euros, days, units
    loss: mean squared error
    output read directly as the estimate
    Classification: the answer is one of a fixed set of categories
    churned / stayed, billing / other, right / wrong
    binary (two classes) or multi-class
    output: a probability, turned into a class with a threshold

    The features do not decide the task. The same two columns can feed a regression (minutes next week) and a classification (churn). The label column changes, so the loss and the use of the output change.

    First question for any "we need a model" request: is the answer a number or a category?
    V13

    Linear regression for business numbers

    prediction = w1 x feature1 + w2 x feature2 + ... + b

    • Each weight says how much the prediction changes when its feature rises by one unit, others fixed.
    • The weights are an explanation as well as a prediction: the first model to try for any business number.
    • Use cases: demand and sales forecasting, price and cost estimation, delivery and processing time, lifetime value, practice minutes.
    • When the relationship is not a straight line, more flexible models exist; V10 states the price of flexibility.
    Sub-topic 4's line is the one-feature case: minutes = w x lessons + b.

    Thresholds, error types and business cost

    truth: churnedtruth: stayed

    • Accuracy = correct / all. Precision = TP / predicted positive. Recall = TP / real positive.
